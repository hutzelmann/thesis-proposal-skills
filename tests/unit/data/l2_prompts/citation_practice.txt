Read the proposal body and ignore the trailing metadata block. In the source
format, `[@key]` renders as a bracketed number and `@key` renders as the
authors' names followed by the number, so `@key` at the start of a sentence is
a narrative citation naming the authors.

The citation practice the guidance asks for:
- When consecutive sentences in one paragraph draw on the same publication,
  the publication is cited once. The run opens with the narrative form, and the
  sentences that follow refer back in words ("they", "their evaluation", "the
  authors", "both studies") instead of repeating the citation.
- A new paragraph that keeps drawing on the source cites it again. So does a
  sentence that follows another source or a claim of the thesis's own.
- Citing a different set of sources on the next sentence (for example `[@a]`
  then `[@a; @b]`, or `[@a; @b]` then `[@b]`) is a change of source, not a
  repetition, and correctly keeps its citation. Only the same set of sources
  on two consecutive sentences, about the same topic, is a repetition.
- A back-reference may point to the authors or to what they built or studied
  ("they", "both studies", "their warnings", "the classifier"), as long as a
  reader can tell which cited work it refers to. A bracketed citation
  followed by such a back-reference is as recognisable as a narrative opener.
- A citation must never be dropped without such a back-reference. A sentence
  that reports a source's content with neither a citation nor a back-reference
  reads as the thesis's own claim, which misattributes it.
- Sentences in which the thesis itself compares, evaluates, or draws a
  conclusion from the works it has just cited ("Both lines of work ignore …",
  "Projects therefore cannot rely on …") are the writer's synthesis, not
  borrowed content. They need no citation, and the synthesis criterion asks
  for them.
- Prior work is grouped by theme and compared within the group. A paragraph
  that walks through one source after another, or spends most of its sentences
  on a single source, is a summary, not a synthesis. Introducing two sources
  in turn and then comparing them is synthesis.
